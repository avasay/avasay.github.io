Copilotification of Microsoft: How a Great Brand Lost Its Meaning

Alejandrio VasayMay 26, 2026

What is Copilot ?

As a developer, I think of Copilot as GitHub Copilot, which is a coding assistant in Visual Studio Code.

That’s still the “clean” version in my head. The one that actually made sense when it first showed up. You’re coding in .NET, you start typing a method, and suddenly the IDE starts finishing your thoughts in a way that feels both impressive and slightly unsettling. That was the moment Copilot actually felt like a product with a clear identity.

Before all the branding explosion, it was easy to explain. GitHub Copilot was the AI pair programmer. It sat in your editor, suggested code, and sometimes saved you from yourself. Simple enough. Coming from 25 years of .NET development, I’ve seen plenty of tooling shifts—from IntelliSense becoming “smart,” to Roslyn analyzers, to cloud-connected everything—but Copilot felt like the first time an AI tool genuinely changed how you write code, not just how you compile it.

Then Microsoft did what Microsoft eventually does with anything that gains traction: it scaled it. Across products. Across teams. Across surfaces. Suddenly Copilot wasn’t just in the editor anymore—it was in chat panels, terminals, browsers, Office apps, Azure dashboards, CRM systems… and somewhere along the way, it stopped feeling like a single thing you could point to and started feeling like a label being stamped onto everything with an LLM behind it.

And that’s where the confusion starts creeping in. Because now when someone says “Copilot,” I don’t immediately know if they mean my coding assistant in VS Code, the thing inside Windows, the one in Excel trying to explain my own spreadsheets back to me, or one of the dozen enterprise variants that all sound like slightly different licensing tiers of the same idea.

It’s not that the technology isn’t impressive—it is. It’s that the name stopped anchoring itself to anything specific. And as someone who’s spent decades watching Microsoft products evolve, that usually means we’re at the point where branding is doing more work than clarity.

So when I ask myself “what is Copilot?” anymore, I don’t get a single answer. I get a whole ecosystem of them.

Copilot as a Marketing Tool

Then, Microsoft discovered that people liked the word “Copilot,” and now it feels like someone inside the company decided every product meeting should end with: “Can we slap Copilot on this too?”

Now my workflow looks like this:

  • GitHub Copilot in VS Code.

  • Microsoft Copilot in Windows.

  • Copilot inside Edge.

  • Copilot inside Word.

  • Copilot inside Excel.

  • Copilot inside Teams.

  • Copilot Studio.

  • Security Copilot.

  • Dynamics Copilot.

  • Azure Copilot.

  • Copilot Pro.

  • Copilot+ PCs.

The weird thing is that I actually like a lot of these tools individually. This isn’t some anti-AI rant. GitHub Copilot genuinely helps me code faster. The AI features in Excel are useful. Some of the Windows integration is nice. The problem is the branding has become so broad that “Copilot” no longer tells me anything meaningful.

Imagine if every Google product was suddenly called Gemini.

  • Gemini Docs.

  • Gemini Chrome.

  • Gemini Android.

  • Gemini Gmail.

  • Gemini YouTube.

  • Gemini Pixel.

Although, I hate that Google puts "Google" on everything too.

Eventually the word stops identifying a product and just becomes corporate AI wallpaper.

That’s where Microsoft is heading with Copilot.

Brand Dilution/Confusion

Even as someone who follows AI and development news obsessively, I still occasionally have moments where I stop and think:  Wait… which Copilot is this again?

Is this the Windows one? The Microsoft 365 one? The paid one? The enterprise one? The coding one? The local AI one? The cloud AI one? The one that needs an NPU? The one built into Edge?

And don’t even get me started on Copilot+ PCs . The first time I heard it, I assumed it meant a laptop that came bundled with Copilot Pro or some premium AI subscription tier. Nope. It’s actually Microsoft’s branding for a category of AI-focused PCs with NPUs for local AI processing. But because the company already used “Copilot” for chatbots, Office features, coding assistants, Windows features, subscriptions, and browser sidebars, my brain was already exhausted by the time the “+” showed up.

Ooh, how about Microsoft Dragon Copilot ? Sounds like an AI assistant that either helps doctors write clinical notes or burns villages in 4K with ray tracing. I genuinely can’t tell anymore.

And what about Copilot Key ? Imagine explaining this to someone in 2012. “Yeah, this button between Alt and the arrow keys instantly summons the corporation’s branded chatbot.” It sounds less like a productivity feature and more like the keyboard equivalent of a sponsored app preinstalled on a budget Android phone.

And how does Copilot in Dynamics 365 Sales differ from Microsoft 365 Copilot for Sales ? A better qestion probably is how does Dynamic 365 Sales even differ from Microsoft 365 Sales , but that's another post for another day.

This is the problem with trying to unify everything under one AI identity.

The word loses precision.

Back when Microsoft had Cortana , at least the assistant had a distinct identity. You knew what it was. It had a personality, a purpose, and a defined role in the ecosystem.

Now “Copilot” feels less like a product and more like a sticker Microsoft applies to any feature containing an LLM somewhere underneath it.

It reminds me of how companies used to shove “smart” into everything during the IoT era.

  • Smart TV.

  • Smart fridge.

  • Smart toaster.

  • Smart toothbrush.

Now we’re entering the “AI Copilot everything” phase.

As a developer, it gets especially funny because half my tools already overlap. I have GitHub Copilot autocomplete suggestions, ChatGPT open in another monitor, Claude open for architecture discussions, and Microsoft trying to inject Copilot into the operating system itself. At some point it starts feeling less like “assistance” and more like every application in my workflow is aggressively trying to become my AI companion.

And the branding spillover creates real confusion for normal users too.

Do You Know If You Are Using Copilot?

I’ve had non-technical friends ask me:

Do I already have Copilot?

Is Copilot free?

Is Copilot just ChatGPT?

Why does my laptop say Copilot+?

Do I need Copilot Pro if Windows already has Copilot?

If your branding strategy requires a flowchart, something has gone wrong.

What’s ironic is Microsoft is actually ahead of a lot of companies in practical AI integration. They moved faster than Apple. Faster than Google in some areas. GitHub Copilot changed developer workflows permanently. The technology itself isn’t the issue.

The issue is that Microsoft seems determined to turn “Copilot” into the AI equivalent of the word “plus.”

Everything becomes Plus. Everything becomes Pro. Everything becomes AI. Everything becomes Copilot.

Eventually the branding starts collapsing under its own weight.

So, What is Copilot?

As someone who lives inside Microsoft products all day, I think the company is approaching the point where the Copilot name is becoming less useful, not more. The more places Microsoft injects it, the less the word actually communicates.

Right now, Copilot basically translates to: This product contains AI somewhere .

That’s not really a product identity anymore.

So, what's Copilot? From my perspective, it’s just a label .
